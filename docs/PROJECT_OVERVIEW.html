Finding large bog-restoration candidates in Ireland

This project used national mapping to create a practical shortlist of large land-title areas that may merit further investigation for bog restoration. It began with mapped bog and peat, removed several unsuitable categories, and then ranked the remaining candidates by the amount of relatively unobstructed bog they contain.

The result is a shortlist of 295 candidate title areas, presented in an interactive map. It is a desktop screening exercise, not a decision that any site is suitable, available, privately owned, or legally accessible.

How the QGIS layers became a shortlist

  1. Set up the national project. QGIS was set to Irish Transverse Mercator (EPSG:2157). Source coordinate systems were checked and temporary repaired copies were used where geometry validation was needed.
  2. Create the bog-core layer. An expression selected raised bog, lowland Atlantic blanket bog, and mountain blanket bog from the 2024 Irish Peat Soils Map. The selection was written to a new national vector layer while retaining bog type.
  3. Check the starting evidence. The raised-bog portion was intersected with NPWS active raised-bog habitat mapping. Calculated areas showed 83.38% overlap. This was a quality check, not an eligibility filter.
  4. Subtract known constraints. Repaired copies of PRA and LDA State assets, SAC boundaries, and NHA boundaries were removed using sequential Difference operations. Only overlapping geometry was cut away, producing the screened-bog layer.
  5. Prepare efficient query geometry. Three large multipart screened-bog features were validated and converted to 21,520 indexed single-part polygons. An area reconciliation retained 623,851 hectares.
  6. Extract intersecting cadastral titles. The query pieces were used to extract local subsets from the national Tailte Éireann freehold and leasehold layers: 249,872 freehold and 1,319 leasehold titles.
  7. Calculate title-level bog area. Each freehold title was intersected with screened bog. Full-title area, screened-bog area, bog percentage, and a leasehold-overlap flag were calculated. Boundary-only contacts were omitted, leaving 249,269 positive-area titles. Filtering for more than 100 hectares of screened bog produced 397 titles.
  8. Build the slope raster. Twenty-seven Copernicus GLO-30 tiles were assembled as a virtual mosaic, projected to EPSG:2157, clipped to the 397-title extent at 30-metre resolution, and converted to percent slope using the Zevenbergen–Thorne method.
  9. Measure low-slope bog. A 0–15% inclusive raster mask was sampled only within each title's exact screened-bog footprint. Filtering for at least 100 hectares of estimated low-slope bog produced the final 295 candidates.
  10. Map commonage, forestry, and clear bog. Current LPIS and historic NPWS commonage geometry were combined without double counting. Commonage and DAFM private forest were intersected with candidates, clipped for display, unioned, and subtracted from screened bog. The remaining 26,235 hectares is described as “clear bog.”
  11. Check access proximity. Each clear-bog geometry was measured to the nearest eligible OpenStreetMap road or track. All 284 assessable candidates were within one kilometre, so the resulting access layer was recorded but not used in ranking.
  12. Prepare the atlas. Candidate boundaries, commonage overlays, and forestry overlays were transformed from EPSG:2157 to WGS 84 and exported as GeoJSON. Feature counts, geometry types, and rank order were validated before publication.

How to interpret the results

  • “Clear bog” is a mapping term used by this project. It means screened bog outside the mapped commonage/private-forest union. It does not mean the land is physically clear, unencumbered, or available.
  • The boundaries are reference data. They do not identify owners or replace a Landdirect title search, legal review, or boundary survey.
  • The slope figures are estimates. The Copernicus product is a 30-metre digital surface model, so trees and structures can affect the result.
  • Commonage and forestry coverage has limits. Historic commonage mapping is not a current legal determination, and the forestry layer does not represent every tree-covered area.
  • Road proximity is not access permission. It says nothing about road condition, a usable entrance, ownership of an approach, or the right to cross land.
  • Desktop screening cannot establish restoration potential. Drainage, erosion, vegetation, hydrology, peat condition, current land use, planning constraints, and stakeholder interest still require investigation.

Recommended next stage

The atlas is best treated as a prioritised list for further due diligence. The leading candidates should be checked against current aerial imagery and Landdirect records, followed by legal-title review, site access checks, ecological and hydrological assessment, and field verification. Source datasets—particularly statutory designations—should be refreshed before any formal decision or publication.
