Finding large bog-restoration candidates in Ireland

This project used national mapping to create a practical shortlist of large land-title areas that may merit further investigation for bog restoration. It began with mapped bog and peat, removed several unsuitable categories, and then ranked the remaining candidates by the amount of relatively unobstructed bog they contain.

The result is a shortlist of 295 candidate title areas, presented in an interactive map. It is a desktop screening exercise, not a decision that any site is suitable, available, privately owned, or legally accessible.

How the shortlist was produced

  1. Map the starting bog area. The 2024 Irish Peat Soils Map was used to identify raised bog, lowland Atlantic blanket bog, and mountain blanket bog. National Parks and Wildlife Service habitat mapping was used as an independent sense-check.
  2. Remove known constraints. Mapped State land and land within Special Areas of Conservation and Natural Heritage Areas were removed from the search area. This identified land outside those mapped categories; it did not prove private ownership.
  3. Match the remaining bog to land-title boundaries. Tailte Éireann cadastral mapping was used to divide the search area into freehold title units. Of the national title dataset, 249,269 titles had some positive-area overlap with the screened bog.
  4. Keep only large bog holdings. Titles containing more than 100 hectares of screened bog were retained. This reduced the list to 397 titles.
  5. Screen for gentler ground. Copernicus GLO-30 elevation data was used to estimate slope at 30-metre resolution. A title qualified if it contained at least 100 hectares of screened bog with a slope between 0% and 15%, inclusive. This produced the final 295 candidates.
  6. Identify mapped commonage and forestry. Current LPIS commonage records, historic NPWS commonage mapping, and the DAFM private forest estate were overlaid on the candidates. Across the shortlist, 26,235 hectares of screened bog lay outside the mapped commonage and private-forest areas. This remainder is described as “clear bog” for ranking purposes.
  7. Check basic road proximity. OpenStreetMap roads and tracks were used for a coarse access screen. Every one of the 284 candidates with at least 0.01 hectares of clear bog was within one kilometre of a mapped road or track, so this test did not help distinguish between candidates and was not added to the ranking.
  8. Publish the shortlist. The web atlas orders all 295 candidates from largest to smallest clear-bog area. A candidate can be selected to centre the map, inspect its boundary, switch between street and satellite imagery, and display mapped commonage or forestry overlaps where present.

How to interpret the results

  • “Clear bog” is a mapping term used by this project. It means screened bog outside the mapped commonage/private-forest union. It does not mean the land is physically clear, unencumbered, or available.
  • The boundaries are reference data. They do not identify owners or replace a Landdirect title search, legal review, or boundary survey.
  • The slope figures are estimates. The Copernicus product is a 30-metre digital surface model, so trees and structures can affect the result.
  • Commonage and forestry coverage has limits. Historic commonage mapping is not a current legal determination, and the forestry layer does not represent every tree-covered area.
  • Road proximity is not access permission. It says nothing about road condition, a usable entrance, ownership of an approach, or the right to cross land.
  • Desktop screening cannot establish restoration potential. Drainage, erosion, vegetation, hydrology, peat condition, current land use, planning constraints, and stakeholder interest still require investigation.

Recommended next stage

The atlas is best treated as a prioritised list for further due diligence. The leading candidates should be checked against current aerial imagery and Landdirect records, followed by legal-title review, site access checks, ecological and hydrological assessment, and field verification. Source datasets—particularly statutory designations—should be refreshed before any formal decision or publication.
