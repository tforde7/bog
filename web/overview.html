What this project is

A national screening exercise, not a declaration of suitability.

This project used national mapping to create a practical shortlist of large land-title areas that may merit further investigation for bog restoration. It began with mapped bog and peat, removed several unsuitable categories, and ranked the remaining candidates by the amount of relatively unobstructed bog they contain.

The result is 295 candidate title areas. Inclusion does not mean that a site is suitable, available, privately owned, legally accessible or ready for restoration.

The QGIS workflow

How the layers became a shortlist

  1. 01

    Set up the national project

    The QGIS project was set to Irish Transverse Mercator (EPSG:2157). Each national source was loaded, its coordinate reference system checked, and temporary repaired copies were used where geometry validation was required.

    Working standard · EPSG:2157 with source geometry preserved

  2. 02

    Create the bog-core layer

    An expression selected three classes from the 2024 Irish Peat Soils Map: raised bog, lowland Atlantic blanket bog and mountain blanket bog. The selected features were written to a new national vector layer while retaining the bog-type field.

    Layer created · Bog core evidence

  3. 03

    Check the starting evidence

    The raised-bog portion of the new layer was intersected with NPWS active raised-bog habitat mapping. Areas were calculated to measure agreement: 83.38% of the NPWS area overlapped the 2024 raised-bog class. This was a quality check, not an eligibility filter.

    QA result · Independent habitat alignment confirmed

  4. 04

    Subtract known constraints

    Geometry-repaired copies of the PRA and LDA State-asset layers, SAC boundaries and NHA boundaries were removed from the bog core using sequential Difference operations. Only the overlapping portions were cut away; an entire bog feature was not discarded because one part met a constraint.

    Layer created · Screened bog outside known State land and SAC/NHA boundaries

  5. 05

    Prepare the screened bog for spatial queries

    The three large multipart screened-bog features were validated and converted to 21,520 single-part polygons. An indexed GeoPackage layer was created so the national cadastral layers could be queried efficiently. An area reconciliation confirmed that the conversion retained 623,851 hectares.

    Layer created · Single-part screened-bog query pieces

  6. 06

    Extract intersecting cadastral titles

    The indexed query pieces were used to extract intersecting records from the national Tailte Éireann freehold and leasehold layers. This produced manageable local subsets: 249,872 freehold titles and 1,319 leasehold titles for exact title-level measurement.

    Layers created · Freehold and leasehold bog-overlap subsets

  7. 07

    Calculate title-level bog area

    Each freehold title was intersected with the screened-bog query pieces. QGIS geometry areas produced full-title area, screened-bog area and bog percentage; leasehold intersection was retained as a flag. Boundary-only contacts were omitted, leaving 249,269 positive-area titles. Filtering screened-bog area > 100 ha reduced the list to 397.

    Layers created · All-title bog metrics and the 397-title size subset

  8. 08

    Build the slope screen

    Twenty-seven Copernicus GLO-30 tiles were assembled as a virtual mosaic, projected to EPSG:2157 and clipped to the 397-title study extent at 30-metre resolution. QGIS/GDAL then derived a percent-slope raster using the Zevenbergen–Thorne method.

    Rasters created · Projected elevation model and percent slope

  9. 09

    Measure low-slope bog within each title

    A raster mask classified cells from 0% to 15% slope, inclusive. The mask was sampled only within each title’s exact screened-bog footprint, and the sampled fraction was applied to its vector bog area. Filtering for at least 100 hectares of low-slope bog retained the final 295 titles.

    Layers created · Slope metrics for 397 titles and the 295-title final subset

  10. 10

    Create the low-slope display layer

    The 0–15% slope result was converted into a polygon layer for the web map. Within each candidate’s screened-bog footprint, cells above 15% slope were removed, leaving the qualifying low-slope portion as a candidate-specific overlay.

    The resulting 295 polygons cover 54,963.87 hectares, compared with the 54,967.96-hectare raster estimate used for screening. The 4.09-hectare national difference comes from conversion at the 30-metre raster-cell edges; no candidate exceeded the checking tolerance.

    Layer created · Candidate low-slope bog overlay

  11. 11

    Map commonage, forestry and clear bog

    LPIS parcels marked as commonage were combined with the historic NPWS commonage layer so their overlap was not double counted. Commonage and DAFM private-forest geometries were intersected with each candidate and clipped for display. Their union was subtracted from the screened-bog footprint to calculate clear bog. Across the shortlist, 26,235 hectares remained outside the mapped union.

    Layers created · Candidate metrics, commonage overlays and forestry overlays

  12. 12

    Check access proximity

    Each clear-bog geometry was measured to its nearest eligible OpenStreetMap vehicle road or land-access track. All 284 candidates with at least 0.01 hectares of clear bog were within one kilometre, so the completed access layer did not distinguish candidates and was not used in the ranking.

    Layer created · Candidate access metrics

  13. 13

    Prepare the public atlas

    Final title boundaries and the three optional overlay layers were transformed from Irish Transverse Mercator to WGS 84 and exported as GeoJSON. Counts, geometry types and rank order were validated. The detailed low-slope geometry is split into candidate-specific files and loaded only when requested. The atlas ranks all 295 candidates by clear-bog area and keeps the full boundary geometry accurate at every map zoom.

    Outputs created · Candidate register and four web-map layers

Read with care

What the results do—and do not—mean

“Clear bog” is a project mapping term. It means screened bog outside mapped commonage and private forest; it does not mean physically clear, legally unencumbered or available.

  • Reference boundaries The atlas does not identify owners or replace a Landdirect title search, legal review or boundary survey.
  • Estimated slope Trees and structures can affect the 30-metre digital surface model used for screening. The displayed low-slope boundary follows the stepped edges of that raster grid.
  • Incomplete overlays Commonage mapping is not a current legal determination, and the forest layer does not include every tree-covered area.
  • Proximity is not permission A nearby mapped road says nothing about condition, an entrance, ownership of the approach or a right of access.
  • Field evidence still matters Drainage, erosion, vegetation, hydrology, peat condition, land use and stakeholder interest require further investigation.

Recommended next stage

Use the atlas to prioritise due diligence.

Leading candidates should be checked against current aerial imagery and Landdirect records, followed by legal-title review, site-access checks, ecological and hydrological assessment, and field verification. Source datasets—particularly statutory designations—should be refreshed before any formal decision.
