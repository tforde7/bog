What this project is

A national screening exercise, not a declaration of suitability.

This project used national mapping to create a practical shortlist of large land-title areas that may merit further investigation for bog restoration. It began with mapped bog and peat, removed several unsuitable categories, and ranked the remaining candidates by the amount of relatively unobstructed bog they contain.

The result is 295 candidate title areas. Inclusion does not mean that a site is suitable, available, privately owned, legally accessible or ready for restoration.

The screening journey

How the shortlist was produced

  1. 01

    Map the starting bog area

    The 2024 Irish Peat Soils Map identified raised bog, lowland Atlantic blanket bog and mountain blanket bog. National Parks and Wildlife Service habitat mapping provided an independent sense-check.

  2. 02

    Remove known constraints

    Mapped State land and land within Special Areas of Conservation and Natural Heritage Areas were removed. This found land outside those categories; it did not prove private ownership.

  3. 03

    Match bog to title boundaries

    Tailte Éireann cadastral mapping divided the remaining search area into freehold title units. Nationally, 249,269 titles had some positive-area overlap with screened bog.

  4. 04

    Keep only large bog holdings

    Titles containing more than 100 hectares of screened bog were retained, reducing the list to 397.

  5. 05

    Screen for gentler ground

    Copernicus GLO-30 elevation data estimated slope at 30-metre resolution. Titles qualified when they contained at least 100 hectares of screened bog on 0–15% terrain. This produced the final 295 candidates.

  6. 06

    Identify mapped commonage and forestry

    Current LPIS commonage records, historic NPWS commonage mapping and the DAFM private forest estate were overlaid. Across the shortlist, 26,235 hectares of screened bog lay outside this mapped union. The project calls this remainder clear bog.

  7. 07

    Check basic road proximity

    OpenStreetMap roads and tracks provided a coarse access screen. All 284 candidates with at least 0.01 hectares of clear bog were within one kilometre of a mapped line, so access proximity did not help distinguish the candidates and was not ranked.

  8. 08

    Publish and rank the shortlist

    The atlas orders all 295 candidates from largest to smallest clear-bog area. It supports boundary inspection, street and satellite basemaps, and mapped commonage and forestry overlays.

Read with care

What the results do—and do not—mean

“Clear bog” is a project mapping term. It means screened bog outside mapped commonage and private forest; it does not mean physically clear, legally unencumbered or available.

  • Reference boundaries The atlas does not identify owners or replace a Landdirect title search, legal review or boundary survey.
  • Estimated slope Trees and structures can affect the 30-metre digital surface model used for screening.
  • Incomplete overlays Commonage mapping is not a current legal determination, and the forest layer does not include every tree-covered area.
  • Proximity is not permission A nearby mapped road says nothing about condition, an entrance, ownership of the approach or a right of access.
  • Field evidence still matters Drainage, erosion, vegetation, hydrology, peat condition, land use and stakeholder interest require further investigation.

Recommended next stage

Use the atlas to prioritise due diligence.

Leading candidates should be checked against current aerial imagery and Landdirect records, followed by legal-title review, site-access checks, ecological and hydrological assessment, and field verification. Source datasets—particularly statutory designations—should be refreshed before any formal decision.
